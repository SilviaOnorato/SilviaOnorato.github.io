# Research

My research focuses on high-redshift quasars: what they tell us about the growth of the first supermassive black holes and about the intergalactic medium (IGM) during the Epoch of Reionization. Below are my first-author projects, from the most recent to the oldest. The full list of my publications is on [ADS](https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/search/fl=identifier%2C%5Bcitations%5D%2Cabstract%2Cauthor%2Cbook_author%2Corcid_pub%2Cpublisher%2Corcid_user%2Corcid_other%2Cbibcode%2Ccitation_count%2Ccomment%2Cdoi%2Cid%2Ckeyword%2Cpage%2Cproperty%2Cpub%2Cpub_raw%2Cpubdate%2Cpubnote%2Cread_count%2Ctitle%2Cvolume%2Cdatabase%2Clinks_data%2Cesources%2Cdata%2Ccitation_count_norm%2Cemail%2Cdoctype&fq=%7B!type%3Daqp%20v%3D%24fq_database%7D&fq_database=(database%3Aastronomy%20OR%20database%3Aphysics)&p_=0&q=author%3A%22Onorato%2C%20Silvia%22%20full%3A%22astrophysics%22&rows=25&sort=date%20desc%2C%20bibcode%20desc&start=0).

## Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua: a close quasar pair at cosmic dawn (2026)

At Gemini Observatory I work with Ema Farina on the _Aether survey_ (PI: E. P. Farina; _Farina et al. 2026 in prep_), which uses JWST NIRSpec/IFU to study quasars at z>6. One of its results is the discovery of **Nāpōwawāʻenakaulua**, a pair of quasars at z≈6.08 separated by only 0.98 arcseconds. This is the most compact quasar pair candidate known in the Epoch of Reionization. We combined spectroscopy from JWST, Gemini/GNIRS and GMOS to characterize both quasars and to measure their black hole masses, accretion rates, and redshifts.

Finding such a close pair is surprising: it is 2–3 orders of magnitude more likely than expected from current models of high-redshift quasar clustering. This may point to a population of quasar pairs triggered by galaxy mergers in the early Universe, or to an unusually dense environment. Either way, the system is a unique laboratory for studying how the first massive galaxies and black holes formed.

The name, given through the [A Hua He Inoa](https://imiloahawaii.org/a-hua-he-inoa) program, means _"Two celestial bodies of fathomless powerful darkness (black holes) in the brilliance of heat reincarnating energy into the Universe"_.

📄 [_Onorato et al. 2026b_](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.31835) (accepted for publication in ApJL & stay tuned for the press release!)

## The Lyα transmission PDF from the E-XQR-30 quasar sample (in prep.)

The last project of my Ph.D. uses the Lyα forest, the absorption imprinted on quasar spectra by neutral hydrogen along the line of sight, to probe the intergalactic medium at the end of Reionization. I am measuring the probability distribution function (PDF) of the Lyα transmission from the high-quality X-Shooter spectra of the E-XQR-30 sample. I then compare it with the PDF obtained from mock quasar spectra based on simulations, with the goal of constraining models of the IGM.

📄 [_Onorato et al._](https://www.overleaf.com/read/hcvrhpyjbwkr#ac6f3d) (in prep.)

## Proximity zones of 59 quasars in the Epoch of Reionization (2026)

The ultraviolet radiation of a quasar ionizes the gas around it, carving out a region known as the _proximity zone_. Its size (R<sub>p</sub>) depends on the quasar's luminosity, on the state of the surrounding IGM, and on how long the quasar has been shining. It is therefore a powerful tool for measuring quasar lifetimes.

I measured proximity zone sizes homogeneously for 59 quasars at 5.77 ≤ z ≤ 7.54, 15 of them for the first time. I expanded the sample to lower redshift with additional data, so that I could study how R<sub>p</sub> evolves. Fitting a power-law model in luminosity and redshift to 100 objects, I found that the luminosity dependence agrees with models, while the redshift evolution is steeper than previously reported. I also identified 13 quasars with unusually small proximity zones. Having ruled out dense absorbers as the cause, I concluded that they likely shine with very short lifetimes of ~10<sup>4</sup> years or less.

📄 [_Onorato et al. 2026a_](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/547/3/stag388/8497464?login=false) (MNRAS)

## Optical and NIR spectroscopy of quasars at z > 6.5: data release and composite spectrum (2025)

For the first part of my Ph.D. project, I built a sample of 45 quasars at 6.50 < z < 7.64. I analyzed optical and near-infrared spectra taken with several instruments (_VLT/X-Shooter_, _Keck/NIRES_, _LRIS_, _DEIMOS_, _Gemini/GNIRS_, _GMOS_, and _LBT/MODS_). I reduced all of them homogeneously with the open-source, Python-based spectroscopic data reduction pipeline [_PypeIt_](https://pypeit.readthedocs.io/en/stable/). It is the largest medium/moderate-resolution sample of z>6.5 quasars observed with ground-based instruments, and it is fully public. It can be used for many science cases: proximity zones, the IGM, and supermassive black hole properties.

I also built a composite spectrum from 33 non-BAL quasars and compared it with others at both high and low redshift. The continuum properties of quasars do not change across cosmic time, even though some emission lines differ in strength.

📄 [_Onorato et al. 2025_](https://academic.oup.com/mnras/article/540/1/1308/8131540?login=false) (MNRAS)

## Multiple populations in the dynamically young globular cluster NGC 2419 (2023)

During my Master's at the _Alma Mater Studiorum University of Bologna_, I studied the distribution of Multiple Populations in NGC 2419. This remote, massive globular cluster (GC) has the lowest dynamical age in the Galaxy, so it is an ideal place to test scenarios for how GCs initially formed and evolved.

I worked with UV and optical photometry from HST (_WFC3/UVIS_) and LBT, using PSF fitting with DAOPHOT, magnitude measurements, and catalog cross-correlations. The filters used are sensitive to differences in light elements (i.e., He, N). Thanks to this, I identified four sub-populations in a _Chromosome Map_ and studied their radial distributions, density profiles, 2D density maps, and the fraction of "first population" (FP) stars. Finally, I compared NGC 2419 with other GCs and with models in terms of the mass segregation of its sub-populations.

I found that "second population" (SP) stars are more centrally concentrated than FP stars, and that the fraction of FP stars is larger than expected. This may be due to the transition of NGC 2419 to a weaker tidal field, as a number of studies suggested it formed in the Sagittarius dwarf galaxy and was later accreted by the Milky Way.

📄 [_Onorato et al. 2023_](https://www.aanda.org/articles/aa/full_html/2023/09/aa46792-23/aa46792-23.html) (A&A)
